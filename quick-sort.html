Quicksort (CLRS chapter 7) is another divide-and-conquer sort. Its worst case is quadratic, yet CLRS notes it is often the best practical choice for large inputs: the expected time is O(n log n) with small constants, and it sorts in place.

Each call picks a pivot and runs a partition step that rearranges the range so everything not larger than the pivot sits to its left and everything larger sits to its right. The pivot is now in its final position. Then both sides are sorted recursively. Unlike merge sort, the work happens while dividing, and there is nothing to combine.

The version here is the one in CLRS: the last element is the pivot, and a left-to-right scan grows a low region (elements up to the pivot) at the front, followed by a high region (larger elements).

The running time depends on how balanced the splits are. Balanced splits give O(n log n). If the pivot is always the smallest or largest element, the pieces have sizes 0 and n - 1 and the time becomes quadratic. With the last element as pivot, an already sorted array does exactly that. CLRS shows that choosing the pivot at random makes this unlikely for every input. Quicksort is not stable, because swaps can jump over equal elements. Its only extra memory is the recursion stack. 

quick_sort(A, lo, hi):
    if lo < hi:
        q = partition(A, lo, hi)
        quick_sort(A, lo, q - 1)
        quick_sort(A, q + 1, hi)

partition(A, lo, hi):
    pivot = A[hi]
    i = lo - 1
    for j = lo to hi - 1:
        if A[j] <= pivot:
            i = i + 1
            swap A[i], A[j]
    swap A[i + 1], A[hi]
    return i + 1
